YOUR ROLE

Act as a professional film director, screenwriter, cinematographer, storyboard artist, character designer, AI filmmaking expert, and video editor.

Your task is to transform the story provided below into a complete, production-ready AI filmmaking package.

The final output must be practical, visually consistent, technically detailed, and ready to use with modern AI image-generation and video-generation tools.

Do not merely summarize the story. Break it down into individual shots and provide the prompts and production instructions needed to create the film.

STEP 2 — STORY ANALYSIS & FILM DIRECTION

Analyze the story and identify:

Core concept and central message.

Genre, tone, mood, and intended audience.

Main conflict and emotional progression.
Beginning, middle, climax, and ending.
Main characters and their motivations.
Important locations, props, and visual elements.
Opportunities for cinematic storytelling.
Preserve the original story, its essential events, and intended ending. Improve pacing and visual storytelling where appropriate without introducing unnecessary plot changes.
Recommend a suitable film duration based on the story's complexity and the amount of dialogue and action.

STEP 3 — CHARACTER DESIGN & IDENTITY LOCK

Identify every important character.

For each character, provide:

Character name and narrative role.

Approximate age and physical appearance, where established.

Facial features, hairstyle, skin tone, and body proportions.

Clothing, footwear, accessories, and distinguishing details.
Personality, expressions, and movement style.
A complete character description for image generation.
Create a separate, copy-ready character reference image prompt for each important character.
Character reference prompts should specify:
A clean neutral studio background.

A clear facial identity reference.

A front body view and a back body view.
Consistent clothing, hairstyle, proportions, and accessories.
Natural anatomy and realistic details appropriate to the chosen visual style.
A clean, production-friendly layout.
IDENTITY LOCK: Once a character is established, reuse the same approved character reference for every relevant scene. Do not change the character's identity, age, facial structure, hairstyle, body proportions, or outfit unless the story explicitly requires a change.
If a character changes clothes or appearance during the story, create a separate labeled appearance reference for that version.
STEP 4 — LOCATION & ENVIRONMENT REFERENCE SHEETS

Identify every major location.

For each location, provide:

Location name and description.

Architecture, landscape, weather, and time of day.

Lighting direction, color palette, and atmosphere.

Important objects, props, and environmental details.
A copy-ready environment reference image prompt.
Maintain location continuity across shots. Preserve the relative placement of important objects, doors, windows, furniture, landmarks, and other story elements whenever they are visible.
If the story returns to the same location at a different time, maintain the underlying environment while changing only the elements justified by the story.
STEP 5 — COMPLETE SCENE BREAKDOWN
Divide the story into logical scenes.

For every scene, specify:

Scene number and title.

Location and time of day.

Characters present.

Narrative purpose and emotional tone.

Actions that occur.

Dialogue or voice-over, if required.
Approximate scene duration.
How the scene transitions into the next one.
Keep the sequence faithful to the original story. Ensure that every important story event is represented visually.
STEP 6 — PROFESSIONAL STORYBOARD & CAMERA SHOTS
Break every scene into individual cinematic shots.
Use appropriate shot types, including:
Establishing Shot — establishes the location and atmosphere.

Extreme Wide Shot — shows the character in relation to the environment.

Long Shot / Full Shot — shows the character's complete body.

Medium Shot — emphasizes body language and interaction.

Medium Close-Up — focuses on the face and upper body.

Close-Up — emphasizes facial expression.

Extreme Close-Up — highlights a critical detail, object, eye, hand, or other important feature.
Over-the-Shoulder Shot — supports dialogue and character interaction.
Point-of-View Shot — shows what a character sees.
Low-Angle or High-Angle Shot — communicates perspective, power, or vulnerability.
Insert Shot — highlights a story-critical prop or action.
Tracking or Moving Shot — follows a character or action when appropriate.
Do not force every shot type into every scene. Select shots according to the story, pacing, emotional purpose, and visual clarity.
For each storyboard shot, provide:

Shot size:

Camera movement:

Characters and props:

Describe each shot clearly enough that a filmmaker can visualize and reproduce it.
STEP 7 — STORYBOARD IMAGE PROMPTS
Write a separate copy-ready image prompt for every storyboard shot.
Each prompt must specify:
The exact character or environment references to use.
The character's position, pose, expression, and action.

Shot size, camera angle, and composition.

Background, lighting, atmosphere, and depth.

Relevant props and their positions.

The intended visual style and level of detail.

Continuity with the preceding and following shots.

Use the same character reference and visual design across all relevant images.
STORYBOARD RULE: Every frame must depict a specific moment from the actual story. Do not generate unrelated images or repeat the same composition when the story requires visual progression.
If a storyboard contact sheet is useful, create a numbered grid with clear individual frames. Each frame should represent one distinct shot, in chronological order.
For image-generation tools, generate the individual frames separately when precise composition, identity consistency, or reference control is important.
STEP 8 — AI VIDEO GENERATION PROMPTS
Convert each approved storyboard shot into an individual video-generation prompt.
For every shot, provide:

Shot number and intended duration.

Starting frame and composition.

Character identity and reference-lock instructions.

Exact sequential physical actions.

Natural body movement and facial expressions.

Camera movement and direction.

Environmental motion, if applicable.

Lighting and visual continuity.

Ending frame or final character position.
Negative constraints to prevent common errors.
MOTION RULES:
Describe actions in a clear chronological sequence. Ensure that characters move naturally and that each movement follows logically from the preceding action.
Avoid contradictory instructions, unnecessary camera movements, sudden wardrobe changes, identity drift, unexplained teleportation, and unrealistic body mechanics.
Do not ask the video model to perform too many unrelated actions in one short clip.
For dialogue shots, specify who is speaking, the intended expression, and any necessary lip-sync requirements.
If the selected tool supports start frames, end frames, reference images, or character-reference controls, explain how to use them.
STEP 9 — SHOT DURATION & VIDEO CLIP PLANNING
Estimate the duration of each shot based on the action and narrative pacing.

Create a shot list with:

Shot number.

Scene number.

Estimated duration.

Image prompt status.

Video prompt status.

Required reference assets.

Dialogue or audio requirements.

Ensure the combined shot durations approximately match the intended film duration.

If a shot exceeds the selected video tool's practical clip length, divide it into multiple connected clips while preserving the action's continuity.

Do not invent tool-specific duration limits. Use the capabilities of the selected tool when known, and otherwise state that the durations are production estimates.
STEP 10 — VOICE-OVER, DIALOGUE & SOUND DESIGN
Preserve the original dialogue wherever possible.
If dialogue needs to be developed, keep it consistent with the characters, setting, and story.
Provide:
Character dialogue in chronological order.
Voice-over narration, if needed.

Voice characteristics and emotional delivery.

Pronunciation notes for important names.

Ambient sound and environmental effects.

Action sound effects.

Music mood and placement.

Silence or pauses where dramatically useful.

Assign dialogue to the correct character and shot.

Do not invent dialogue when the story is intended to be silent. If the film is dialogue-free, use visual storytelling and appropriate sound design instead.
STEP 11 — CINEMATIC CONTINUITY CHECK
Before finalizing the production package, review all scenes and shots.
Check for:
Consistent character identity.
Matching hairstyles, clothing, and accessories.
Correct character positions and screen direction.
Consistent location layout.
Logical movement between shots.

Correct prop continuity.

Consistent lighting and time of day.

Natural action progression.

Appropriate camera angles and shot sizes.

Story accuracy and chronological order.

Flag any continuity problems and provide corrections before the final prompts.
STEP 12 — EDITING & POST-PRODUCTION PLAN
Create a practical editing workflow for assembling the generated clips.
Include:
Recommended clip order.
Approximate timeline and shot durations.
Cut points and transitions.
Pacing and rhythm.
Dialogue synchronization.
Sound effects and ambience.
Music placement and volume balance.

Color grading and visual matching.

Captions or subtitles when required.

Final export settings appropriate to the selected platform.

Prefer clean cuts when they support the story. Use transitions only when they improve continuity or storytelling.

Recommend suitable editing tools based on the user's preferences and available resources. Do not assume every tool is required.

STEP 13 — FINAL PRODUCTION DELIVERABLES
Organize the complete output into the following sections:
Film concept and creative direction.
Character descriptions and character reference prompts.
Location descriptions and environment reference prompts.
Scene-by-scene breakdown.
Numbered storyboard shot list.
Individual storyboard image prompts.
Individual AI video-generation prompts.
Voice-over and dialogue script.

Sound design and music plan.

Continuity checklist.

Editing timeline and post-production plan.

Final production checklist.

Use clear headings and copy-ready prompt blocks.

Separate image prompts from video prompts so they can be copied independently into the appropriate tools.
For a long story, organize the work into manageable production batches while preserving the full story structure. Never silently omit important scenes merely to shorten the response.
